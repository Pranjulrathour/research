# Chapter 4 — Work: the shape of the next decade

Chapter 3 gave you four channels. This chapter asks how they will play out in the places most readers of this book actually stand: at the start of a career, in a country whose economy was built on exporting skilled labour, in roles that the exposure studies put near the top of the list. It does not forecast. It lays out three scenarios, shows what each implies, and spends most of its time on the one problem that is the same in all three: the entry level.

## Three scenarios, not one forecast

Nobody knows how fast AI capability will improve or how fast organisations will adopt it, and the honest way to plan under that uncertainty is to hold several scenarios at once and ask what is robust across them.

**Slow diffusion.** Capability improves steadily but adoption is gated by reliability, regulation, liability and the sheer slowness of organisational change. This is the electricity pattern from chapter 3: the technology exists for years before most workplaces are rebuilt around it. In this scenario, the 2030 workplace looks like the 2026 workplace with better tools: most jobs persist, tasks shift at the edges, and the people who learn the tools well have an advantage that compounds. The risk is complacency, because the first few years look like nothing is happening.

**Uneven diffusion.** Adoption is fast in some sectors and slow in others, for reasons that have little to do with technical feasibility. Software, marketing, customer support, back-office finance and parts of law move quickly because their output is digital, their errors are recoverable and their buyers are cost-sensitive. Healthcare, infrastructure, education and government move slowly because their errors are not recoverable, their buyers are regulated and their workflows are entangled with institutions. In this scenario, the labour market splits: some professions are transformed in five years while others barely notice, and the biggest risk is being trained for a fast-moving profession with slow-moving skills.

**Fast diffusion.** Capability jumps, reliability crosses the thresholds that gate autonomy, and adoption follows within a few years because the economics are overwhelming. Whole task categories are automated, including many that the exposure studies rated as merely augmentable. New tasks appear but not fast enough to absorb the displaced, at least for a while. This is the scenario in which the first channel dominates, and it is the one that most public discussion assumes. It is possible. It is not the only possibility, and planning as if it were certain is as much a mistake as planning as if it were impossible.

The question to ask of any decision is: does it hold up in all three? Learning to evaluate AI output holds up in all three. Building deep skill in a slow-moving, high-stakes domain holds up in all three. Specialising in a task that is already exposed and hoping adoption stays slow holds up in one.

## The entry-level paradox

There is one problem that appears in every scenario, and it bears directly on students.

Professions have always trained their juniors by giving them the routine work. The first-year law associate reviews documents and drafts standard clauses. The junior analyst builds the spreadsheet and formats the deck. The junior developer fixes small bugs and writes the tests. The junior journalist rewrites press releases. These tasks are not valuable because of what they produce; they are valuable because doing them, under supervision, is how a person learns the judgment that the senior work requires.

The tasks that train juniors are precisely the tasks that current AI systems do best: well-specified, routine, pattern-matched against thousands of prior examples. So the first-channel pressure falls hardest on the rung of the ladder that people climb onto first. If a firm can get the document review done by a system, it hires fewer first-years. If it hires fewer first-years, it has fewer people learning to become the partners it will need in fifteen years. The firm's short-term incentive and its long-term need point in opposite directions, and firms are not good at resolving that tension in favour of the long term.

The early evidence from 2023 onward is consistent with this. The most careful study so far used payroll records covering millions of US workers to track employment by age and occupation from late 2022, when the current tools became widely available. For workers aged 22 to 25 in the occupations most exposed to AI (software development and customer service among them), employment fell by about 13 per cent relative to less-exposed occupations over the following two and a half years, while employment of older workers in the same occupations was stable or rising.[^1] The decline was in headcount, not wages, which is what you would expect if firms were hiring fewer juniors rather than paying them less, and it was concentrated in occupations where the technology is used to *do* tasks rather than to *assist* with them. Studies of job postings over the same period found entry-level software and analyst postings falling faster than senior ones, and reports from the large technology employers showed new-graduate hiring well below its 2019 and 2022 levels. The pattern is not yet conclusive, varies by country and sector, and could be partly a hangover from the over-hiring of 2021, but it is the pattern the framework predicts, and anyone finishing a degree between now and 2030 should plan as if it continues.

How do institutions respond? Three adaptations are already visible.

*Fewer, broader hires.* Firms that cut junior headcount tend to raise the bar for the juniors they do take, looking for people who can already do some of the mid-level work: judgment, client interaction, system-level thinking. The junior role becomes more like what the second- or third-year role used to be.

*Apprenticeship redesign.* Some firms and professions are rebuilding how they train, replacing routine work with supervised use of the tools, structured review of machine output, and faster exposure to real decisions. This is the right response and it is rare, because it costs the senior people's time.

*A wider gap between those who get in and those who do not.* When the entry rung narrows, the people who clear it do well and the people who do not face a harder path. The variance of outcomes for a graduating class widens.

The implication for a student is uncomfortable and clear: the degree gets you to the door, and the thing that gets you through it is evidence that you can already do work that is not routine. Chapter 10 is about how to build that evidence. For now, the point is that the paradox is structural, it is not your fault, and it rewards people who understand it early.

## India specifically

India's modern economy was built, more than most, on exporting skilled human labour: software services, business process outsourcing, and more recently the global capability centres through which multinational firms run engineering, finance and analytics teams out of Indian cities. Those sectors employ several million people and account for a large share of the country's services exports. They are also sectors whose core tasks (writing code to specification, processing transactions, handling support tickets, maintaining systems) are among the most exposed in every study.

That exposure is a threat and an opportunity at once, and which one it becomes depends on choices that are being made now.

The threat is value migration. The IT-services model sells hours of competent labour at a price advantage. When a system can do the routine hours, the price advantage narrows and the buyer either pays for fewer hours or moves the work up the value chain to where human judgment still commands a premium. Firms that respond by selling the same hours cheaper lose. Firms that respond by selling outcomes, systems and judgment can grow, because the demand channel from chapter 3 is strong in software: when building software gets cheaper, the world wants far more of it.

The opportunity is that India has, in the same decade, the world's largest cohort of young graduates, a digital public infrastructure (identity, payments, data exchange) that most countries lack, and a domestic market of over a billion people with needs that are underserved in their own languages. The country that staffs the world's AI-assisted back office is one future. The country that builds for its own billion users, and then exports what it built, is another. Both are possible; they require different things of a graduate.

For the graduate, the practical version is this. The service-sector entry-level job of 2026 is the most exposed job in the country, and the entry-level paradox applies to it with full force. The skills that hold up are the ones that are scarce on both sides of the value migration: understanding a business domain deeply enough to specify what should be built, designing and operating systems rather than writing code to someone else's specification, and working in the gaps between what the tools can do and what the customer actually needs. Chapter 11 returns to India's decade in full.

## A worked case: one graduating class

Make it concrete. Consider a class of MCA or B.Tech graduates leaving an Indian college in 2026, and follow the three scenarios through their first four years.

In **slow diffusion**, the services firms hire roughly as they did, perhaps a little less, at roughly the same salaries. Most of the class joins a large firm, spends six months in training and two years on maintenance, testing and support work. The tools are present but used as assistants; the routine work is still done by people, with help. By 2030 the class looks like the class of 2018 with better tooling. The students who used those years to learn a domain and to build systems rather than write to specification are ahead, but not dramatically, and the ones who did not are employed. The risk in this scenario is that it feels safe right up to the point at which the next scenario arrives.

In **uneven diffusion**, which is the scenario most consistent with the evidence so far, the large services firms cut fresher intake substantially, as several did in 2023 and 2024, and raise the bar for those they take: the campus interview now includes system design and a review of real work. The global capability centres keep hiring but for roles that used to go to people with two years' experience. The class splits. Perhaps a third clear the raised bar and start on work that is immediately non-routine; a third take longer, through smaller firms, startups or contract work, to reach the same place; a third struggle, because the rung they were trained for is gone and nobody told them in time. The students with a portfolio of verifiable work (chapter 10) are disproportionately in the first third, regardless of their college's ranking, because the raised bar is exactly the evidence they have.

In **fast diffusion**, the cut in junior hiring is sharp across sectors, the GCC roles narrow too, and the first two years after graduation are hard for most of the class. The same portfolio still separates the ones who are hired, but fewer are hired. The students who do best are the ones who go toward the new-task channel directly: building and operating AI systems for Indian businesses, in Indian languages, for problems the global products do not address (chapter 11), where demand is rising fastest and competition is thinnest.

Three things hold across all three. The portfolio of verifiable work is the deciding evidence in every scenario. Domain depth and systems skill are rewarded in every scenario. And the student who waits for the market to tell them which scenario is coming finds out at the campus interview, which is the most expensive possible way to learn it.

## What a decade of this looks like from inside

A last point about time, because the scenarios can feel abstract.

If you are twenty-two in 2026, the next decade takes you to thirty-two. Across those ten years, in any scenario, the tools you use at work will change several times, the tasks you are paid for will shift, and at least one skill you spent years acquiring will become cheap. That is not a prediction about AI; it was already true for anyone who entered the workforce in 1996 or 2006. What AI changes is the rate, and the rate matters because it compresses the number of times you have to reinvent part of what you do into a shorter span.

The people who did well through previous transitions were not the ones who guessed the technology right. They were the ones who kept a layer of skill that transferred (judgment, communication, understanding of how things fit together), kept learning the new tools fast enough to stay useful, and stayed close enough to real problems to notice when the ground moved. None of that requires knowing which scenario is coming. All of it requires starting before you have to.

## What would make this chapter wrong

If by 2031 entry-level hiring in exposed professions has recovered to its pre-2023 trend, the paradox was a transition effect that institutions solved, and this chapter overstated its persistence. Check junior posting data and early-career wages in software, law, consulting and finance.

If India's IT-services and GCC sectors have grown in headcount and in revenue per employee through the decade, the value-migration threat was managed and the chapter's warning about the service-sector entry job should be read as having been heeded. If headcount has fallen while revenue held, the migration happened and the warning stood.

If the fast-diffusion scenario arrived, this chapter's even-handedness between scenarios will look like hedging, and the reader should go directly to chapter 10 and chapter 12.

## What to do this year

Identify the one skill in your target role that is hardest to automate and get measurably better at it. "Measurably" is the operative word: find a way to produce evidence (a shipped system, a published analysis, a result someone else can check) rather than a certificate. If you cannot name that skill, that is the first task: talk to three people five years ahead of you in the role and ask what they are paid for that a tool cannot yet do. Their answers will agree more than you expect.
